Effective Resource Utilization: Stop Collecting, Start Doing

March 2025 · 7 min read · Tags: Tips, Productivity

There's a specific kind of person who is extremely busy learning data analytics without actually learning very much.

Their bookmarks folder has forty-seven items. Their Notion database has a "Resources" section with three hundred entries, colour-coded by topic, with completion status fields that mostly say "To Do." They've started six Udemy courses. They follow every major data science account on LinkedIn and save every post about SQL tips or pandas tricks.

They feel productive because they are constantly processing content about data analytics. But processing content about a skill and acquiring the skill are different activities that feel similar and are not.

If this description is even partially accurate, this post is for you.

The Trap Has a Name

Benjamin Franklin is credited with the observation that an investment in knowledge pays the best interest. What he didn't say — because it wasn't a problem in 1750 — is that collecting resources about knowledge is not the same as acquiring knowledge.

The bookmarking trap works like this: encountering a resource triggers a small dopamine response. You've "done something" about the gap you've identified. The anxiety of not knowing SQL is temporarily relieved by bookmarking a SQL tutorial. The tutorial doesn't get opened, but the anxiety is managed.

Repeat this process several hundred times and you have a meticulous collection of resources and a skills gap that hasn't moved.

The One Resource Rule

The fix is not motivational. It's structural.

For any skill you're currently learning, commit to exactly one resource at a time and finish it before starting another.

Not two. Not "one primary and one supplementary." One.

This rule feels unnecessarily restrictive until you apply it, at which point you realise that most of the anxiety driving resource accumulation disappears when you have a single, active, in-progress resource. There's no decision to make about what to study next. There's no FOMO about missing a better course. There's only: finish the thing you started.

The specific resource matters far less than this structure. A mediocre resource that you finish beats an excellent resource that sits at 23% completion.

The 48-Hour Rule

Finishing a resource is not sufficient. Retention without application is low.

The second structural rule: within 48 hours of completing any learning unit — a course section, a tutorial, a chapter — build something with what you learned.

Not a practice problem from the same course. Not a quiz. Build something that applies the skill to a question you care about or a dataset you found yourself.

The specificity here matters. "I'll practice" is a plan that doesn't execute. "Within 48 hours of finishing the pandas groupby section, I'll run a groupby analysis on the NEPSE trading data I already have" is a plan that does.

The 48-hour window is deliberate. Longer than 48 hours and the activation energy to start rises to the point where most people don't start. Within 48 hours, the knowledge is still active in working memory and the application cost is lowest.

Setting a Deadline Before You Start

Most people start courses without a target finish date. This is the same as starting a project without a deadline — the work expands to fill indefinite time, then gets abandoned when something more urgent appears.

Before you start any learning resource, set a specific finish date. Not "I'll finish it this month." A specific date. Write it somewhere visible.

Then work backward: how many units per day do you need to complete to hit that date? Does that fit your schedule? If not, adjust the date — but set one.

A learning schedule without a deadline is a wish list.

The Audit: What to Do With Everything You've Already Collected

If you already have forty-seven bookmarks and six in-progress courses, the audit is uncomfortable but necessary.

Go through everything and ask one question per item: Am I currently using this or will I use it within the next two weeks?

If yes: it stays, and gets scheduled.

If no: it gets deleted.

Not archived. Not moved to a "maybe later" folder. Deleted.

This is the part that feels wasteful. It isn't. A resource you've bookmarked and will never use is consuming zero value and a small amount of cognitive space every time you see it. Deleting it is a net positive.

After the audit, you should have one active resource. Everything else is gone.

The Resource Selection Problem

Once you commit to the one-resource rule, resource selection becomes a real decision rather than a compulsive activity. You can only pick one, so it should be the right one.

For any skill, the selection criteria in order of priority:

  1. Is this the right skill for where I'm going? No resource selection problem is worth solving if the skill itself isn't aligned with your current goal.
  2. Does this resource cover the specific depth I need? Surface-level familiarity requires a different resource than interview-level mastery.
  3. Is the format one I actually complete? If you don't finish video courses, don't buy video courses. If you finish books, buy books. Your track record is the only honest signal here.
  4. Have people similar to me completed it and found it useful? Check forums, not star ratings. Five-star ratings include people who gave five stars after watching 10%.

What "Doing" Looks Like vs. "Collecting"

The distinction is simple in principle and easily confused in practice.

Collecting: Reading about pandas, bookmarking pandas tutorials, watching pandas videos, following pandas accounts, joining pandas communities.

Doing: Writing a pandas script that loads a real dataset, cleans it, runs an aggregation, and outputs a result.

The collecting activities feel productive because they involve data analytics content. The doing activity produces a concrete output — something that exists after you stop working, that you can show someone, that required you to confront the gap between what you understood in theory and what you could actually execute.

Confusion and frustration during the doing activity is the signal that learning is happening. Comfort and ease during collecting activities is the signal that it isn't.

The Scorecard

At the end of each week, answer three questions:

  1. What did I finish this week (in terms of learning resources)?
  2. What did I build this week that I didn't build last week?
  3. What have I deleted from my resource backlog this week?

If the first two answers are "nothing" and the third is also "nothing," the week was spent collecting. The purpose of the scorecard isn't guilt — it's accurate feedback. You can't optimise what you don't measure.

Takeaway

The information is not the constraint. There has never been more free, high-quality material for learning data analytics than exists right now.

The constraint is doing. Pick one resource. Finish it. Build something with it within 48 hours. Then pick the next one.

The bookmark folder is not a learning strategy. It's anxiety management. Delete most of it and start one thing today.
